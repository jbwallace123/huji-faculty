Kempner Institute x The Edmond & Lily Safra Center for Brain Sciences NeuroAI Collaboration

Recent advances in machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) are transforming science, technology, medicine, and the global economy. Yet, despite impressive progress, current AI systems still fall short of the flexibility, efficiency, and robustness of human intelligence.

Against this backdrop, a new interdisciplinary research domain—NeuroAI—has emerged, dedicated to the systematic integration of neuroscience and artificial intelligence. NeuroAI seeks to foster a two-way exchange of ideas, methods, and research questions that advances both our understanding of the brain and the design of intelligent machines.

The NeuroAI Initiative

Building on deep expertise in NeuroAI and an already warm relationship, the Kempner Institute for the Study of Natural and Artificial Intelligence at Harvard University and the Edmond & Lily Safra Center (ELSC) for Brain Sciences at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem (HUJI) have established a joint NeuroAI initiative focused on the fundamental connections between natural and artificial intelligence.

To create a vibrant and sustained interchange between the two academic communities, the NeuroAI Initiative is launching an initial set of interactive activities designed to promote the exchange of ideas, data, and results, and to identify concrete, high-impact projects for future collaborative research.

Vision

We encourage collaborations between Kempner and ELSC researchers that advance research at the intersection of neuroscience and AI and facilitate a two-way exchange of expertise: using AI to understand biological intelligence, drawing on insights from brains and behavior to improve artificial systems, and developing theories or methods that apply across both.

We are interested in a broad range of collaborative projects spanning neuroscience, cognitive science, and artificial intelligence. Projects may use AI and machine learning to advance our understanding of the brain, draw on biological intelligence to develop new approaches to AI, or investigate principles of learning and computation that apply across natural and artificial systems.

Releveant topics may include:

  • Learning and adaptation. Investigating how biological and artificial systems learn and adapt to changing environments, and how principles of learning and adaptation can be transferred between neuroscience and AI.
  • Neural and artifical representations. Exploring how information is represented in biological and artificial systems, and how insights into neural representations can inform the design of AI systems and vice versa.
  • Perception, memory, decision-making, and behavior. Investigating the mechanisms underlying perception, memory, decision-making, and behavior in biological systems, and how these insights can inform the development of AI systems and vice versa.
  • Neural and model dynamics. Examining the dynamic properties of neural and artificial systems, including how activity evolves over time and how these dynamics support learning, memory, and decision-making, and how insights from neural dynamics can inform the design of dynamic AI models and vice versa.
  • Biologically inspired architectures and learning rules. Investigating the circuit, and system-level mechanisms underlying learning and memory in biological systems, and how these insights can inform the development of AI systems that mimic or leverage similar mechanisms.
  • Robust and efficient intelligence. Investigating how biological and artificial systems achieve robust and efficient intelligence, and how principles and methods can lead to new insights and innovations in both biological and artificial systems.
  • Embodied and multi-agent systems. Investigating how biological and artificial systems interact with and adapt to their environments, both individually and in groups, and how principles from embodied and multi-agent systems can inform the design of AI systems and vice versa.
  • AI-enabled analysis of both neural and behavioral data. Leveraging AI and machine learning techniques to analyze neural and behavioral data, and how insights gained from these analyses can inform the development of efficient and generalizable systems in the other domain.

We welcome experimental-computational collaborations, theoretical work—including work complemented by empirical validation—and projects that involve the development of shared tools, datasets, models, or benchmarks.

Projects do not need to fit neatly within one of these areas. We are especially interested in collaborations that bring together complementary expertise from the two institutions and would be difficult for either research group to pursue independently.
